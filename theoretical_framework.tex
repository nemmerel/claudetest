An agent born at time $\tau$, with a lifespan of $M$ years, optimizes lifetime utility $U(c_\tau, c_{\tau+1},...,c_{\tau+M-1})$, with $U(\cdot)$ concave and increasing. Her flow budget constraint is given by equation \ref{eq:agent_bc}. At each time $t$, the agent chooses between consumption $c_t$ and purchasing two types of assets. The first is a capital asset, $K_{t+1}$, which is purchased at price $q_t$, depreciates at rate $\delta$, and yields a rental rate $\rho_t$. The second asset is a financial asset, $S_{t+1}$, purchased at price $X_t$, which pays a dividend of $d_t$. The agent works, and receives labor income of $w_t l_t$. Initial asset holdings are zero, and the agent leaves no bequests. 

\begin{align}
\label{eq:agent_bc}
c_t + q_tK_{t+1} + X_t S_{t+1}=  q_t(1-\delta)K_t + S_t (X_t + d_t)  + \rho_tK_t +  w_t l_t 
%\forall \tau \leq t \leq \tau + M -1 \nonumber \\
%K_{\tau} = S_{\tau} = K_{\tau+M} = S_{\tau+M}=0 \nonumber
\end{align}

\noindent There are two distinct types of income implicit in this budget constraint. 

\begin{definition} \label{def:nat_inc} National income is equal to income from wages plus rental income from capital plus dividends from securities, minus depreciation.

\be
\label{eq:nat}
Y^n_t = w_t l_t + \rho_t K_t - \delta q_{t-1}K_t + d_t S_t.
\ee

\end{definition}

National income is income received from production. If we sum equation \ref{eq:nat} over all US residents,  definition \ref{def:nat_inc} is in line with the BEA definition of national income, which, absent measurement errors, equals the national production of output.\footnote{See the BEA Handbook, \citet{fox2009concepts}.}

\begin{definition} \label{def:kg} Capital gains equal the return on the price index times the value of the asset held.  Then $KG^S_t \equiv S_t X_{t-1} \frac{X_t - X_{t-1}}{x_{t-1}}$. \end{definition}

We include capital gains as part of the income measure because they affect the stock of resources individuals have available to consume, save, or pass on as bequests. Capital gains enable higher spending directly through asset sales and indirectly by relaxing collateral and borrowing constraints. It is straightforward to show that \textit{holding returns constant}, capital gains increase welfare by the marginal utility of consumption. 

Equally important to accounting for capital gains is that it better distinguishes welfare differences between investors who have preferences for different asset types. If not, the Dividend Stock holder will (incorrectly) be regarded as better off than an investor in Growth Stocks, and a Coupon Clipper who holds only bonds will be seen as better off as as a company founder who grows its share value. 

The BEA definition of National Income explicitly excludes capital gains, which are not earned in production. Indirectly, however, capital gains are present through the retained earnings of corporations. Under a standard Miller and Modigliani (1961) assumption, a firm that retains a dollar of earnings increases its market value by \$1. Indirectly, then, at least some capital gain income is accounted for in the national accounts income measure.

To isolate capital gains from other income flows, we define ordinary factor income as national income minus the retained earnings of corporations, and \textit{pure} capital gains as capital gains minus retained earnings. 

Figure 2 compares capital gains to other capital income sources in the post-war era. Pure capital gains are large, especially in the post-1980 period, where they average 10\% of national income. Retained earnings, the component present in national income, averaged 4\% of national income for the same period. 

What are the source of pure capital gains? In Appendix G, we theoretically show that even absent any shocks positive capital gains will be present in a growing economy. In addition, changes in earnings driven by an increase in monopoly power, a growing share of earnings to the corporate sector, or unmeasured intangible investment can drive revaluations. A final source of variation in capital gains is changes in interest rates. Lower interest rates generate higher asset prices (and positive capital gains), since future dividends are discounted at lower rates. We discuss the impact of lower interest rates (and more generally, changes in discount rates) in Section J. 

Haig-Simons income is a comprehensive income concept that includes changes in asset values as well as standard income components. We measure this here as ordinary factor income plus pure capital gains. When measured on a post-tax post-transfer basis, it equals an individual's consumption plus their change in wealth. Formally, Haig-Simons income for individual $i$ in year $t$ is:
\be
Y^{HS}_{it} = Y^{ordinary factor}_{it} + KG_{it} = C_{it} + \Delta W_{it}
\ee
where $KG_{it}$ is total capital gains, $C_{it}$ is consumption, and $\Delta W_{it}$ is the change in net worth. Equivalently, Haig-Simons income equals national income plus pure capital gains.

At the individual level, income differs based on whether it is pre or post tax. `Pre-Tax' Haig-Simons income, following PSZ, is income before the tax and transfer system, with the exception of the operation of private and public pensions, as well as disability and unemployment insurance. `Post-tax' Haig-Simons income equals pre-tax income, minus taxes, plus all forms of government spending: cash-transfers, in-kind transfers, and collective consumption expenditures. The Pre-Tax Haig-Simons income is useful for measuring the effective tax rate faced by individuals, while post-tax Haig-Simons income is closest to approximating an individual's consumption plus change in networth. In addition to these income concepts, we explore the robustness of our results by adding our capital gains series to other income measures: the \textit{market income} of PSZ, the comprehensive income of Larrimore et al., expanded income of the IRS, and AGI minus realized capital gains. 

Household wealth is the market value of fnancial and nonfnancial assets held by households, minus the market value of their liabilities. We largely follow the Financial Accounts in determining the asset and liability composition of the balance sheet, with the exception of two asset classes where we provide bespoke estimates: private business wealth and tenant occupied real estate. Our primary measure (excepting private business and tenant real estate) is equal to the Financial Accounts net worth for households and nonprofts, minus the net worth of nonproft organizations, minus consumer durables.

Following PSZ, our basic unit of analysis is the individual, with income split equally across spouses.

GDP $Y_t$ is produced by firms that produce output by hiring labor and capital, making profits of $\Pi_t = Y_t - w_t L_t - \rho_t K_t$.\footnote{These are thus `pure' or factorless profits.} All capital is owned by the business sector, but individual firms rent the capital used in their own production from other firms. Net business income ($BI_t$) equals rental income plus profits minus depreciation: $BI_t \equiv \Pi_t + \rho_t K_t - \delta K_t$. 

The market value of firms $V_t$ equals the value of the capital stock, $K_t$, plus the market value of the present discounted value of pure profits: 

\begin{align}
V_t = K_t + E_t\left[\sum_{s=1}^{\infty} M_{t,t+s} \Pi_{t+s}\right]$,
\end{align}
where $M_{t,t+s}$ is the stochastic discount factor.

GDP $Y_t$ is produced by firms that produce output by hiring labor and capital, making profits of $\Pi_t = Y_t - w_t L_t - \rho_t K_t$.\footnote{These are thus `pure' or factorless profits.} All capital is owned by the business sector, but individual firms rent the capital used in their own production from other firms. Net business income ($BI_t$) equals rental income plus profits minus depreciation: $BI_t \equiv \Pi_t + \rho_t K_t - \delta K_t$. 

National income $NI_t$ equals wages plus business income plus depreciation, and is (theoretically) equal to GDP. It does not contain any explicit capital gain component. 

The market value of firms $V_t$ equals the value of the capital stock, $K_t$, plus the market value of the present discounted value of pure profits: $V_t = K_t + E_t\left[\sum_{s=1}^{\infty} M_{t,t+s} \Pi_{t+s}\right]$, where $M_{t,t+s}$ is the stochastic discount factor. 

The capital stock evolves with the law of motion $K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)K_t + I_t$. Investment equals corporate savings (retained earnings $RE_t$), plus depreciation, plus personal savings $S_t$. Retained earnings equal business income minus dividends, $RE_t = BI_t - D_t$. Personal savings equals wages plus dividends minus consumption, $S_t = w_t L_t + D_t - C_t$. Since the capital stock is owned by the business sector, personal savings are invested through net equity issunces $EQ_t = S_t$. 

Capital gains are defined as the change in market value of firms minus net equity issuances: $V_{t+1} - V_t - EQ_t$. They can be a function of:
\begin{itemize}
\item A change in the expected value of future profits $\Pi_{t+s}$
\item Retained earnings $RE_t$ reinvested in the firm
\item Changes in the stochastic discount rate $M_{t+s}$
\end{itemize}
