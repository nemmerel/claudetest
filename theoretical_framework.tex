\section{Theoretical Framework}

Aggregate output $Y_t$ is produced by a representative firm with production function $Y_t = F(K_t,L_t)$, where $K_t$ is capital and $L_t$ is labor. Firm profit equals $\Pi_t = F(K_t,L_t) - w_t L_t - \rho_t K_t$, where $w_t$ is the wage rate, and $\rho_t$ the rental rate of capital.\footnote{These are thus `pure' or factorless profits, as in \citet{karabarbounis2019accounting}.} All assets are owned by the business sector, and business income ($BI_t$) includes both (implicit) capital rental income as well as profits: $BI_t \equiv \Pi_t + \rho_t K_t - \delta K_t$. Out of this income, firms either pay dividends $d_t$ or retain the earnings $RE_t = BI_t - d_t$. 

Capital evolves through the law of motion $K_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t + I_t$. Investment is the sum of retained earnings (gross of depreciation) and net equity issuances, $NE_t$, which equals share issuances minus buybacks. We then have $I_t = RE_t + \delta K_t + NE_t$. 

The market value of the firm (share price $P_t$ times shares outstanding $S_t$) equals the value of its capital plus the present discounted value of future profits, denoted by $V_t$.
\be
\label{eq:pdef}
P_t S_t = K_{t+1} + E_t[\sum_{s=1}^{\infty} M_{t,t+s} \Pi_{t+s}] \equiv K_{t+1} + V_t,
\ee
where $M_{t,t+s}$ is the stochastic discount factor. Implicit in this equation is the \citet{miller1961dividend} assumption that a dollar of retained earnings increases the value of the firm by a dollar.

We furthermore assume that equity issuances and buybacks are at the prevailing market price, $NE_t = P_t (S_t - S_{t-1})$. This ensures that changes in net equity have no effect on the price per share. \footnote{To see this, define shareholder value as $W = P S$ and consider a deviation in net equity raised $\Delta$, at a price per share of $P$. Then new firm value is $W' = W + \Delta$, and new shares outstanding is $S' = S + \Delta/P$. The price per share is then $W'/S' = (P S + \Delta) / (S + \Delta / P) = P$.}

There are two distinct types of income present in this economy. 

\begin{definition} \label{def:nat_inc} (Net of depreciation) National Income is equal to income from wages plus dividends plus retained earnings.
\be
\label{eq:nat}
Y^n_t =  w_t L_t + d_t + RE_t 
\ee
\end{definition}

National income is income received from production. Definition \ref{def:nat_inc} is in line with the BEA definition of national income, which, absent measurement errors, equals the net national product: $Y^n_t   = Y_t - \delta K_t$.\footnote{See the BEA Handbook, \citet{fox2009concepts}.}

\begin{definition} \label{def:kg} Capital gains equal the real increase in the price per share of equity times the value of equity. Defining $r^p_t = P_t / P_{t-1} - 1$, $KG_t = P_{t-1} S_{t-1} \cdot r^p_t$. \end{definition}
\noindent Capital gains are the increase in wealth caused by changes in asset prices. From Equation \ref{eq:pdef}, the law of motion for capital, and the share repurchase equation, we can decompose gains into corporate retained earnings and changes in the value of future discounted profits: 
\be
\label{eq:kgb}
KG_t = \Delta V_t + RE_t.
\ee

The BEA definition of national income explicitly excludes capital gains, which are not earned in production. However, as shown in Equation \ref{eq:kgb}, capital gains are indirectly present through retained earnings. To isolate capital gains from other income flows, we define ordinary factor income as national income minus the retained earnings of corporations: $Y^{o}_t = Y^n_t - RE_t$, and \textit{pure} capital gains as capital gains minus retained earnings, $KG^p_t = KG_t - RE_t$. 

To measure aggregate capital gains in the data, we use data from the Financial Accounts of the Federal Reserve, which collects information on the balance sheet and asset revaluations of the U.S. household sector. We convert nominal to real gains by subtracting net national product inflation, and separate total from pure capital gains by subtracting the BEA series for corporate savings. 

Figure 2 displays the results. Capital gains are large, especially in the post-1980 period, where they average 14\% of national income. Pure capital gains are the majority, averaging 10\% of national income, while retained earnings, the component present in national income, averaged 4\%. The large magnitude and persistence of capital gains suggests that analyses that do not include this income source provide an incomplete picture of inequality in recent times.

The growth in the economic importance of capital gains motivates a comprehensive income definition that includes changes in asset prices as well as standard income components. 
\begin{definition} \label{def:hs} Haig-Simons income \citep{haig1921concept, simons1938personal} equals ordinary national income plus capital gains: $HS_t = Y_t^{o} + KG_t$. \end{definition} 

%\noindent As described by \citet{hicks1939value}, Haig-Simons income ''is what [one] can consume during the week and still expect to be as well off at the end of the week as at the beginning.''   , and it is sometimes described as the ``gold standard'' income measure (see \citet{jct2012} or \citet{auerbach1989capital}).

Compared with other income definitions, Haig-Simons has several desirable properties. 
\begin{remark} \label{remark:hs_sav} The change in household wealth, $W_t = P_t S_t$, between two periods is equal to Haig-Simons income (on a post-tax post-transfer basis) minus consumption: $\Delta W_t = HS_t - c_t$. \end{remark} 
\noindent To see this, we introduce a household sector that holds all assets and receives all income, with flow budget constraint given by 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:agent_bc}
c_t +  P_t S_t = S_{t-1}P_t + d_t +  w_t L_t  
\end{align}
Subtracting $W_{t-1}$ yields $\Delta W_t = (Y_t^n - c_t) + KG_t^p$, i.e. national savings plus pure gains. Haig-Simons income can thus more accurately account for changes in wealth compared to national income and national saving. 

Capital gains are also closely related to consumer welfare.
\begin{remark} \label{remark:hs_inc}  From the household's budget constraint, capital gains and other capital income sources are interchangeable: a dollar of gains expands the agent's feasible consumption set identically to a dollar of interest or dividends.   \end{remark} 

In Appendix J we formally show that holding expected future returns constant, a dollar of capital gains increases welfare by the marginal utility of consumption. For this reason, when studying relative inequality, Haig-Simons is preferred to other income concepts since it better distinguishes welfare not only between asset holders and non-asset holders, but between investors who hold different types of assets. Consider two investors with identical wealth invested in assets earning the same total return. Investor A holds a bond paying a 5\% coupon; Investor B holds a growth stock that pays no dividend but appreciates 5\% per year. Under national income, Investor A earns \$5 per \$100 invested, while Investor B earns zero — despite being economically identical. More broadly, any income measure that excludes capital gains will systematically understate the income of growth stock holders relative to dividend stock holders, equity holders relative to bond holders, and business founders relative to salaried executives.

%This distinction is quantitatively important. As seen in Figure 2, capital gain income has grown relative to other capital income sources. At the top of the wealth distribution, this bias is especially pronounced, since private and public equity wealth is concentrated in the top 10\% of the distribution. 

What are the sources of aggregate capital gains, and why have they grown over time?
\begin{remark} \label{remark:hs_src} From Equations \ref{eq:pdef} and \ref{eq:kgb}, capital gains may be driven by (i) changes in firm profits, (ii) changes in retained earnings, or (iii) changes in discount rates.  \end{remark} 

\noindent In the U.S. context, there is substantial evidence that all three channels have played a role in the rise in capital gains. For (i), \citet{grullon2019us,autor2020fall} document a secular increase in concentration and market power, which in turn has led to increases in both earnings and profits \citep{barkai2020declining,eeckhout2024value}. 

%As shown from Equation \ref{eq:pdef}, one important long-run driver is firm profits, which drive changes in $V_t$. In the U.S. context, a growing literature documents a secular increase in concentration and market power \citep{grullon2019us,autor2020fall}, which in turn has lead to increases in earnings and profits \citep{barkai2020declining,eeckhout2024value}. 

For (ii), a large literature has documented a secular increase in intangible investment in the U.S.,\footnote{See, for example, \citep{crouzet2022economics,peters2017intangible}.} which in turn has been capitalized into higher stock market valuations \citep{hall2001stock,mcgrattan2010unmeasured}. These investments, however, are often unrecorded under GAAP and national income accounting standards \citep{lev2001intangibles,corrado2009intangible}.\footnote{Although the BEA has revised its treatment of intangible investment to include additional categories such as software, it still excludes firm-specific human capital, organization capital, brand equity, and advertising.} As a result, measured corporate profits and retained earnings are lower than their true values. If intangible investment is undermeasured in the national accounts but observed and valued by asset markets, it would show up in our measurement as an increase in $KG_t^p$. 

Outside of these secular shifts, standard trend-growth also generates capital gains. Under a balanced growth path in which all variables grow at rate $g$, asset prices will also follow this trend, and the ratio of capital gains to GDP will be $g \cdot \frac{W}{Y}$. Plugging in the value of public and private equities held by US households for $W$ and a real GDP growth rate of 2\% yields a back-of-the-envelope estimation of 5\% of national income in capital gains per year. 

For (iii), there has been a decline in real interest rates and estimates of the natural rate of interest in the United States \citep{eggertsson2019model,laubach2016measuring} and globally \citep{rachel2019secular}. Changes in discount rates complicate the interpretation of Haig-Simons income because in addition to the standard wealth effect of higher asset prices, there is a separate welfare effect of lower future returns. We discuss the impact of lower interest rates (and more generally, changes in discount rates) in Section J. 

\begin{remark} \label{remark:payout}  Changes in firm payout policy can affect total capital gains $KG_t$, but not national income or pure gains $KG^p_t$. As a result, Haig-Simons income is payout irrelevant. \end{remark} 
\noindent From Equation \ref{eq:kgb}, changes in stock buybacks \textit{holding dividends fixed} have no effect on $KG_t$, since $RE_t$ is unaffected. If firms substitute buybacks for dividends, however, $RE_t$ and $KG_t$ increase. In contrast, both pure gains $KG_t^p$ and national income are unaffected, since $KG_t^p$ excludes retained earnings and national income is pinned down by the production side of the economy. Since its two subcomponents are payout irrelevant, Haig-Simons income is as well. 

\begin{remark} \label{remark:doublec} The Haig-Simons concept does not double count income. \end{remark} 
\citet{CanberraGroup2001,splinter2025distribution} argue that Haig-Simons double counts income, since higher expected future earnings $E_t \Pi_{t+s}$ count once initially when stock prices increase (higher $\Delta V_t$), and again when earnings are paid out (higher $d_{t+s}$). This analysis, however, overlooks the fact that paying out dividends reduces capital gains one-to-one with distributions; this is exactly the reason why stock prices fall on ex-dividend dates. The fact that future dividend payments subtract from capital gains ensures that the increase in future earnings is only counted once.\footnote{A similar argument to this was made in  \citet{larrimore2021recent}.}
